# Paper Writing Prompt

Apply these instructions to abstracts, introductions, related work, methods, experiments, results, discussions, and conclusions.
The goal is for readers to understand the content and its logical relationships on their first reading.
Prioritize clear reasoning, natural transitions, and accurate expression before refining the language.

## 1. Clarify what you want to express before choosing the wording

- Before writing, identify the question that the current subsection and paragraph should answer.
- Develop each paragraph around one central idea, with every sentence serving that idea.
- Arrange content according to its logical relationships, rather than the order in which ideas occur to you.
- Remove sentences that do not advance the explanation or argument, even if they sound professional in isolation.
- Do not force every paragraph into the same template; let the content determine its structure.

## 2. Let each sentence advance the discussion

- Build each sentence on information already introduced, then add the new information that is needed.
- Make the relationship between adjacent sentences clear, whether it is explanation, elaboration, illustration, comparison, cause and effect, a condition, a qualification, or a generalization.
- Do not introduce several new concepts in succession without explaining how they relate to one another.
- When moving from an observation to its cause, from a method to its results, or from results to their interpretation, state the necessary intermediate evidence or reasoning rather than leaving readers to supply it.
- Retain useful repetition of concepts; do not replace terms arbitrarily just to avoid repetition.
- Create coherence primarily through continuity of content; use transition words only to mark relationships that actually exist.

## 3. Explain information in the order readers need it

- When a concept, symbol, or step first appears, provide the explanation needed to understand it.
- Introduce the object, purpose, or necessary background before describing specific operations and details.
- When describing a method, explain what it does, why it is needed, and how its steps connect; include only design rationales that help readers understand it.
- When using an equation, explain what it describes, its variables, and its role, and connect it to the surrounding discussion.
- When describing results, distinguish observed facts, interpretations of those facts, and inferences drawn from them.
- Do not require readers to revisit several passages to understand the basic meaning of the current sentence.

## 4. Use concrete, direct sentences

- Give each sentence one main claim, placing necessary conditions and qualifications close to that claim.
- Wherever possible, make clear who or what performed an operation and what result it produced.
- Prefer concrete nouns and precise verbs; reduce stacks of abstract nouns, layers of modifiers, and vague evaluations.
- Use pronouns sparingly and make the referents of this, these, it, which, and similar expressions clear. When a pronoun refers to an object mentioned more than one sentence earlier, or intervening sentences introduce other possible referents, repeat the specific noun or established term rather than leaving readers to infer the reference.
- Use first-person expressions such as I, we, my, and our sparingly in academic papers. Prefer the relevant method, experiment, result, or other concrete object as the subject when it expresses the same meaning clearly. Do not introduce awkward passive constructions merely to avoid first person.
- Let the content determine sentence length. Split complex sentences when they are hard to understand, and clarify the connections between short sentences when needed.
- Do not treat sophisticated vocabulary, complex syntax, or dense terminology as measures of scholarly quality.

## 5. Match claims to their supporting evidence

- Support every important claim with an appropriate explanation, reference, data, or derivation.
- Keep conclusions within the scope of the objects, conditions, and experimental settings covered by the evidence.
- Distinguish facts, assumptions, design choices, experimental observations, and interpretations rather than blending them together.
- Do not present correlation as causation, a limited result as a universal rule, or a plausible conjecture as a verified conclusion.
- Retain necessary qualifications, but avoid repetitive, vague, or layered hedging.
- Identify missing support rather than using fluent prose to conceal gaps in the argument.

## 6. Maintain consistency throughout the manuscript

- Use consistent names for the same concept, and distinguish related concepts with different meanings.
- Ensure that research objectives, method descriptions, experimental metrics, and final conclusions align with one another.
- Make the progression between subsections clear, avoiding abrupt changes in the subject of discussion.
- When earlier material needs to be repeated, restate only what readers need at the current point; do not repeat large portions of the background, methods, or results.
- End paragraphs by completing the current idea or naturally leading into what follows; do not mechanically add inflated statements of significance or generic summaries.

## 7. Maintain a natural, restrained academic tone

- Use the bundled [humanizer skill](humanizer/SKILL.md) to check for formulaic language while preserving academic accuracy.
- Remove empty openings, promotional language, repeated emphasis, and unsupported claims that inflate the contribution.
- Do not pile up words such as Furthermore, Moreover, and Importantly to create an appearance of coherence.
- Do not mechanically use three-item lists, negative contrasts, or fixed paragraph structures.
- Do not deliberately add colloquialisms, emotion, rhetorical flourishes, or unnecessary syntactic variation merely to make the text sound less AI-generated.
- Aim for clarity, naturalness, and accuracy rather than showcasing the author's linguistic skill.

## 8. Revision principles

- Make the smallest changes needed to achieve the goal, preserving the original scientific meaning, data, equations, citations, technical boundaries, and existing edits unrelated to the task.
- Fix logical ordering, missing transitions, ambiguous references, and redundant wording before refining vocabulary and grammar.
- Do not add unsupported facts, causal relationships, or contribution claims to improve fluency.
